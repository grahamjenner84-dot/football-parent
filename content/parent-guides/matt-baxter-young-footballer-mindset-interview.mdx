## Young Footballer Mindset: A Conversation With Matt Baxter

Most of what a football parent worries about happens off the ball. The quiet car journey after a bad game. The child who used to be first in the car and now drags their feet. The release letter. The day they say they want to quit.

Football Parent put eight of those moments to **Matt Baxter**, a youth athlete mindset coach who works one to one with young players, and asked what he actually sees happening in a child's head, and what a parent can do about it. His answers are below, in his own words.

## Rebuilding a Sense of Self After Release

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
When a player is released, what have you seen help a child rebuild their sense of self in the first few weeks?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

The first thing I'd say is that it isn't only a football setback. For a lot of academy kids, "I'm an academy player" has become who they are. When that label goes, they lose part of their identity, not just a place in a team. So the rebuild starts by separating the person from the badge.

In the first few weeks I'd let the child be upset and listen to them, let them process it and have their feelings heard. There's no need to rush to "everything happens for a reason." Grief that gets hurried tends to come back later.

Once things settle, help them see who they are underneath the club they represented. What do they love about the game? What kind of player are they when they're at their best? What qualities got them into an academy in the first place? None of those were taken away. They are part of who they are as a player.

It also helps to get them playing again somewhere they're wanted and can enjoy it. Confidence comes back through enjoyment and love of the game.

## The First Ten Minutes After a Bad Game

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
When a child comes off the pitch upset after a bad game, what's really happening emotionally in those first ten minutes, and how can a parent help?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

In those first ten minutes the emotional part of the brain is in charge. The child is flooded with disappointment, embarrassment, and often worry about what you, their coach and their teammates think. The thinking part of the brain, the bit that can reflect and learn, isn't available yet. That's why analysis in the car park, or getting straight into the post-match debrief in the car, so often ends in tears, crossed words or silence.

So the best thing a parent can do is almost nothing, done well. Be calm and warm. A hug, a drink, a simple "I loved watching you play" and then quiet. Let them lead. If they want to talk, listen more than you speak. If they don't, that's fine.

The review can come later, ideally that evening or the next day, and it should come from them. Ask questions rather than giving your verdict: "What went well? What would you do differently next time?" A child who reaches their own answer learns far more than one who's handed yours.

## Why Some Players Bounce Back and Others Spiral

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
What separates a child who bounces back from a bad game from one who spirals, and what can a parent on the touchline actually influence?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

It rarely comes down to talent. It comes down to what the child does with the next moment. Young players who bounce back have learned that a mistake is an event, not a verdict on them. Players who spiral carry each mistake into the next action, and then the next, until the whole game feels like proof they're not good enough.

I teach players a Next Action Reset: notice the mistake, let it go, and put all their focus on the very next thing they can control. I also teach them to catch the chain from thought to feeling to performance. "That was rubbish" becomes tension, which becomes another mistake.

From the touchline, a parent influences more than they realise, mostly through their body language. Kids look over. If they see a head in hands or hear sideline instructions, the spiral speeds up. If they see a calm parent who's still smiling, it slows down. Cheer effort and bravery, stay quiet on the technical stuff, and let the coach coach.

## Where Real Confidence Comes From

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Where does real confidence in a young player come from, and what well-meaning parent behaviour quietly undermines it?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

Real confidence is built on evidence and the story they tell themselves, not on being told "you're brilliant". It comes from preparation, from remembering difficult moments they've handled before, from seeing themselves improve, and from knowing who they are as a player.

With players I work with, I help them build an evidence-based Confidence Identity: who they are on the pitch at their best. This goes with them to every game and every training session, so their confidence isn't left to chance or dependent on external validation from a parent, a coach or a teammate.

The well-meaning behaviour that undermines it most is over-praise tied to outcomes. "You were the best player out there" feels lovely, but it teaches a child that their worth depends on results. On the day they're not the best, the confidence goes with it. Praise effort, choices and courage instead:

- "I loved how you kept demanding the ball after that missed chance."
- "Your reaction and effort to win the ball back after you lost it was fantastic."
- "You were really brave on the ball and kept trying to make good decisions."

The other one is fixing everything for them. When parents step in too quickly, the child never gets to discover that they can cope, and that discovery is where durable confidence comes from.

## Getting Through a Plateau

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
When a child hits a plateau and gets frustrated, how much of that is mindset versus ability, and what actually helps them through it?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

Plateaus and even dips are a normal and important part of development. There are so many factors that affect a young player's development at different stages. Growth spurts, stepping up an age group, more physically developed opposition: all of these create periods where progress seems to stall. Ability is usually still developing underneath. What turns a plateau into a problem is how the child interprets it.

A fixed mindset says, "I've stopped getting better, so maybe I'm not good enough. I was better than them before, and now they're better than me." A growth mindset says, "This is hard right now, and when it feels hard is where improvement happens. Keep going."

One of the simplest tools I use is the word YET. "I can't beat that defender" becomes "I can't beat that defender yet." It's a small change, but it moves the child from judgement back to learning.

What helps practically:

- **Shrink the focus.** Instead of "get better", pick one specific thing to work on for the next few weeks and notice small wins.
- **Look back.** Help them look back at where they were a year ago, or talk about a different challenge they've overcome in the past. Success leaves clues, and they can be reapplied to the new challenge.
- **Keep enjoyment in the mix and praise effort.** Frustrated children often start training out of fear instead of love, and that slows progress even further.

## The Early Signs of Burnout

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
What are the early signs a young player is heading for burnout rather than just a dip, and what should a parent do first?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

A dip is temporary and usually linked to something specific: negative thought patterns getting in their way, a change of circumstances they're adapting to, a challenging week at school, a new coach.

Burnout builds more slowly and spreads wider. The early signs I look for:

- **Loss of joy.** A child who used to be first in the car now drags their feet.
- **Constant tiredness and niggling injuries.**
- **Irritability.**
- **Dreading training**, rather than just being nervous about games.

Another telling sign is when the language shifts from "I want to" to "I have to."

What should a parent do first? Notice, and ask gently, without an agenda. "You seem a bit flat about football lately. How are you finding it?" Then listen. Very often the answer points to load: too many teams, too much training, no free time, too much pressure.

The first fix is usually reducing volume and bringing back free play and rest, not pushing harder. A break isn't the end of a career. For many young players, it can be what saves one.

## When Your Child Talks About Quitting

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
When a child starts talking about wanting to quit, how should a parent respond, and how do you tell a passing wobble from a real sign they've had enough?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

The most important thing is not to react in the moment, either with panic or with "we've paid for the season, you're not quitting." Both shut the conversation down. Stay curious: "Tell me more. What's making you feel that way?"

A passing wobble usually has a specific trigger: a bad game, falling out with a teammate, being dropped, a coach who shouted. It comes out straight after an emotional moment and fades within days. When you dig in, the child still loves playing. They just don't love how they feel right now.

A real sign they've had enough is consistent over weeks, isn't tied to one event, and shows up alongside a general loss of enjoyment. Sometimes they're asking for something else entirely, like a different sport, more time with friends, or a break.

Either way, the child needs to feel heard, and one of the most underrated skills as a parent is to listen properly. If it's a wobble, helping them work through it teaches resilience. If it's real, respecting it protects their relationship with sport for life. Children who are allowed to step away often come back. Children who are forced to stay rarely do in the long run.

## Encouragement vs Pressure

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Where's the line between healthy encouragement and pressure that backfires, and what does it look like when a parent is pushing their own ambition onto a child?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Matt Baxter • Youth Athlete Mindset Coach
</p>

A simple test: who is the goal for? Healthy encouragement supports the child's own goals and love of the game. Pressure starts when the parent's hopes, investment or identity become the thing driving it.

A few signs to watch for in yourself:

- The car journey home is a performance review.
- Your mood depends on their result.
- You talk about "we" when you mean their career: "We need to get signed this year." You would be surprised how much I hear that.
- You know more about their stats than about how they felt playing.
- You find yourself comparing them to other kids in the team.

Children are brilliant at picking up on this, even when nothing is said out loud. They start playing not to disappoint, playing to prove something to someone rather than playing to enjoy, and that fear shows up in their performance.

I say this with a lot of empathy, because as a football parent myself I know how easy it is to care too much. It comes from love. But the most powerful thing you can give a young player is the sense that your love and pride don't depend on how they played. One of the most important things you can say after a game or a training session is: "I love watching you play."

## Final Thoughts

Across all eight answers, Matt keeps coming back to the same idea: a young player's confidence has to belong to them. It can't rest on a club's name, a result, a parent's mood on the touchline or being told they were the best player out there, because all of those can disappear on a bad day.

For parents, that turns into a short list of things that are actually in your control. Say less in the [first few minutes after a match](/parent-guides/what-to-say-after-football-matches) and let the review come from your child later. Watch your body language on the touchline as closely as they do. [Praise effort, choices and courage](/football-development/build-confidence-young-footballers) rather than outcomes. Treat a [plateau](/football-development/why-isnt-my-child-improving-at-football) as normal, and a steady loss of joy as a reason to reduce [load](/football-development/football-burnout), not add to it. And if your child is [released](/academy-pathway/understanding-academy-release), or talks about quitting, listen first and decide later.
