## Martin Brock on the Junior Premier League: Part 2

In [Part 1 of our interview](/parent-guides/jpl-martin-brock-interview-part-1), Martin Brock, Chief Executive of the [Junior Premier League](https://www.juniorpremierleague.com/england/), explained what the JPL is, how it differs from grassroots football, and what parents should expect around trials, training, travel and cost.

In Part 2, we press him on the harder questions: how the JPL stops competitive results from crowding out development, whether playing in the league genuinely helps a child get noticed, the misconceptions parents bring with them, how parents should behave on matchdays, and how a family should actually decide between grassroots and the JPL.

## How Does the JPL Balance Results With Long-Term Development?

<InterviewQuestion>How does the JPL encourage clubs to balance competitive results with long-term development?</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewAnswer name="Martin Brock" org="JPL" photoSrc="/images/people/martin-brock-jpl-320.jpg">

This is the central tension in all youth football, and anyone who claims to have fully solved it isn't being straight with you. Winning matters, learning to compete is part of developing a young player. But if winning becomes the only thing, development suffers: the same strong players play every minute and the others drift.

So we build the balance into the rules rather than just hoping for it. At U11s and below, we run a 66% minimum game-time rule; at U12s and above, a 50% minimum. No child is there to warm a bench, they're there to play, thrive and develop. We also break matches into thirds at U11s and below, and quarters at U12s and above, which gives coaches more natural moments to make considered substitutions and get real coaching points across during the game, rather than only at half-time.

Beyond the rules, we encourage a culture that values how players are developing, not just the league table: resilience, mastery, treating every game as a chance to learn. The clubs that get this right are the ones who produce the players who go on to something bigger.

</InterviewAnswer>

<div className="my-8 rounded-[14px] border-[3px] border-[#201E1D] bg-[#E3EDFF] px-[22px] pt-[18px] pb-1 shadow-[5px_5px_0_#201E1D]">

<h3 className="m-0 mb-2.5 flex items-center gap-2.5 font-display text-[21px] font-extrabold leading-[1.2] text-[#201E1D]"><img src="/parent/icon/parent-icon-160.webp" alt="" className="h-9 w-9" />Football Parent perspective</h3>

A minimum game-time rule is only as good as how a club applies it. It's a reasonable question to ask a club directly at trial stage how minutes are actually shared out across a squad in practice.

## Does Playing in the JPL Improve a Child's Chances of Being Scouted?

<InterviewQuestion>What is the relationship between the JPL, professional academies and scouts? Does playing in the JPL genuinely improve a child's chances of being noticed?</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewAnswer name="Martin Brock" org="JPL" photoSrc="/images/people/martin-brock-jpl-320.jpg">

I'll answer this carefully, because it's where parents most want a big promise and most deserve honesty. Playing competitively against strong opposition genuinely helps a young player develop, and players developing well, in good games, are more likely to be seen than those in a quieter setting. Around one in eleven of our players goes on to play in the professional game, a figure we're proud of, and one we back up by sharing those success stories constantly, because families deserve to see they're real.

We also create genuine opportunities to be seen: scout-attended showcase events, All-Stars selection, and high-level fixtures. But I want to be clear about what we are and aren't. We are not a scouting agency, and we would never guarantee any individual child exposure or an academy place, anyone who promises that is misleading you.

What we offer is the environment where development and visibility become possible. Scouts do watch JPL football. But the right reason to join is to become a better player in better games. The opportunities tend to follow.

</InterviewAnswer>

## What Are the Most Common Misconceptions Parents Have About the JPL?

<InterviewQuestion>What are the most common misconceptions parents have about the JPL?</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewAnswer name="Martin Brock" org="JPL" photoSrc="/images/people/martin-brock-jpl-320.jpg">

Three come up again and again. The first is that the JPL is a guaranteed route to a professional career. It isn't, and we'd never claim it is: it's an environment where talented, committed players develop and can be seen, but there are no guarantees in football, and anyone selling certainty is selling a myth.

The second is that it's uniformly elite everywhere, that every team, every age group, every region is at the same high level. Honestly, that's not yet the case, and it's exactly what we're working hardest to put right through a clearer tiered structure. I'd far rather a parent hear that from me than feel let down on the pitch.

The third runs the other way: that the JPL is only for future stars and there's no point unless your child is exceptional. Not true, it's for ambitious players who want to be stretched, whatever their ultimate ceiling. If your child wants better football and is ready to commit, there's a place in the JPL worth exploring.

</InterviewAnswer>

## What Role Should Parents Play on Matchdays and Around Training?

<InterviewQuestion>What role should parents play on matchdays and around training?</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewAnswer name="Martin Brock" org="JPL" photoSrc="/images/people/martin-brock-jpl-320.jpg">

The best thing a parent can do is be supportive, present and calm, and then let the coaches coach and the children play. Matchdays should be about the players, not the touchline. The parents who help most encourage every child, respect the referee even when it's hard, and make the car journey home about whether their child enjoyed it, not about mistakes or the scoreline. That last one matters more than people realise; how you are in the car after a game shapes whether a child keeps loving football.

Around training, the most valuable role is quieter still: getting them there, backing the coach, and letting your child own their own development rather than managing it for them. We take conduct seriously, because the environment around a young player is part of their experience of the game, and it reflects on every family there.

The simplest guide I can offer: be the parent your child is glad to see on the touchline, not the one they're anxious about.

</InterviewAnswer>

## Grassroots or JPL: How Should a Family Decide?

<InterviewQuestion>What advice would you give a family deciding between grassroots and a JPL opportunity?</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewAnswer name="Martin Brock" org="JPL" photoSrc="/images/people/martin-brock-jpl-320.jpg">

The first thing I'd say might surprise you: it usually isn't a choice between the two. More than half of JPL players still play for their local grassroots team as well, because playing alongside their friends matters, and we'd never want to take that away. For a lot of children, the JPL is extra football, not a replacement: more coaching, more competitive games, a bigger stage, on top of the grassroots football they love.

So rather than "grassroots or JPL," the real question is usually "does my child want more?" If they're hungry for extra challenge, extra coaching and tougher games, and the family's realistic about the added time and travel, the JPL is where that player genuinely thrives. And if your child is happiest simply playing locally with their mates, that's wonderful too, and the door to us is always open when they want more.

The best decisions in youth football are child-led. If your child is the one asking for more football, come and find out how far they can go; you don't have to give up anything they already love to do it.

</InterviewAnswer>

## Final Thoughts

Across both parts of our interview, Martin Brock has been consistent about where the JPL stands: an environment built for ambitious players who want more competitive football, sitting alongside grassroots for most families rather than replacing it, with no guarantee of professional success attached.

In this second part, he's been direct about the limits of what the JPL can offer around scouting, honest about the misconceptions parents bring, and clear that most JPL families never actually give up grassroots football to take part. His advice on matchday conduct, that the car journey home matters more than the scoreline, is one worth holding onto regardless of which league your child plays in.

To learn more about the JPL, its clubs and its programmes, visit the [Junior Premier League](https://www.juniorpremierleague.com/england/).
