## Late Developers In Girls Football

Players develop at different rates. Some don't stand out at 11, don't get picked at 13, or join a development programme later than some of their peers.

If your daughter is talented and motivated but not yet in the frame, this article is for you.

Late development is a normal part of how children grow, and it plays a particularly big part in girls' football.

---

## What "Late Developer" Actually Means

The phrase "late developer" gets used in a few different ways, and they mean different things.

**Physical late development** refers to girls who mature later than their peers and are smaller, lighter or less physically developed at a given age. In sport, physical maturity is frequently confused with ability, which means later-maturing players are persistently undervalued in talent identification.

**Technical and footballing late development** refers to players whose technical ability, decision-making, or football intelligence comes on at a different pace. Some haven't had the early coaching, some needed more time, and for some the game clicked later.

**Confidence-related late development** refers to players who have the ability but whose belief in themselves lags behind, on the pitch, at trials or whenever they're being watched. This matters more in girls' football than people often acknowledge.

These three types of development often overlap. A physically late developer who is consistently overlooked may develop confidence issues. A technically capable player who's been written off early may disengage.

---

## The Role of Puberty

Puberty affects almost every part of girls' football development, and its effect is often underestimated.

Girls typically begin puberty between ages 8 and 13, with significant variation. Changes in height, body composition, coordination and physical capacity don't all arrive at the same time. A girl's centre of gravity shifts. Her limb proportions change. Skills she performed fluently might suddenly feel awkward as her body adjusts.

**This is normal and temporary, and it says nothing about long-term ability.**

It does mean that a player who looked technically assured at 11 might go through a period at 12 or 13 where nothing quite feels right. She may lose pace compared to peers who've had their growth spurt earlier. Her coordination may feel disrupted. Her confidence can take a hit.

This phase is sometimes called the **adolescent awkward phase** in sports science, and it's particularly pronounced in skill-based sports. Players and parents who understand what's happening can get through it more calmly. Players who don't understand it may draw the wrong conclusions about whether they're any good.

## The Physical Maturation Bias in Talent Identification

Research into talent identification in youth sport consistently shows that physically mature players are significantly overrepresented in development programmes.

In girls' football, this means:

- Girls who enter puberty early tend to be faster, stronger, and more physically imposing than peers of the same age
- Coaches and scouts sometimes mistake physical superiority for long-term talent
- Later-maturing players, who may be technically superior, get overlooked

The relative age effect (the tendency to favour players born earlier in the school year) is well-documented in boys' football. It applies in girls' football too, although findings vary between studies, and maturity bias appears especially important.

In practice, **missing out in early development rounds often reflects physical maturation rather than ability.**

## Why Good Players Sometimes Get Missed

Parents often assume that selection decisions are precise and objective. Talent identification is harder than that.

Coaches are making decisions based on limited observation opportunities, incomplete information and players who are developing at different rates.

A player may be overlooked because she is smaller, quieter, less physically mature, recovering confidence, or simply because the timing was not right. None of those factors automatically reflect long-term potential.

Selection processes involve uncertainty, and good coaches know that some talented players will emerge later than others.

## The Confidence Dimension

Confidence affects girls' football more than people often recognise.

Confidence in sport is partly physical. A player who feels strong and capable in her body will take more risks, be more assertive and play more freely. Physical changes in early adolescence can disrupt this, particularly for girls who've built their sporting identity around being physically able.

Environment matters too. Research into female athletes consistently shows that the relational environment is particularly significant for girls' development: how coaches communicate, whether mistakes are treated as learning or criticism, and whether effort is valued alongside outcome.

A girl in a high-pressure environment where mistakes are punished or selection is competitive and opaque may pull back, play it safe, and appear less capable than she is. The same player in a supportive environment, trusted by her coach, may play freely and show qualities nobody had seen.

**If your daughter is going through a difficult phase, look closely at her environment.** A new club, coach or setting sometimes turns a struggling player into one who is hard to recognise.

## What Parents Can Do

The instinct when your daughter seems to be falling behind peers is to do more: more coaching, more sessions, more trials. Sometimes that's right, but think it through first.

What tends to help late developers most:

**Continue playing:** it sounds obvious, but girls who feel they've fallen behind their peers sometimes want to stop. Keep football enjoyable and easy to get to before you think about optimising it.

**Find the right environment:** a setting that develops confidence, values effort and gives consistent game time can do more for a late developer than a technically superior programme where she feels exposed or marginalised.

**Be patient without being passive:** keep making good decisions about where she plays and who coaches her, without forcing premature conclusions.

**Don't create comparison pressure:** tracking which of her peers is in which programme, and which friends got trials, adds weight without helping her develop. Keep the focus on her own growth.

**Separate your anxiety from hers:** parents of late developers often worry a lot about what's being missed, and some of that worry is fair. If it passes to her, it adds to the pressure she already feels. She needs you to stay calm.

## The Long-Term Picture

The girls' pathway has real openings for late developers. This is covered in more detail in our guide on [how Girls Football Academies Work In The UK](/girls-football/how-girls-football-academies-work). Unlike the boys' EPPP system, where early registration and compensation fees can create structural barriers to late entry, the girls' game remains more accessible to players who emerge later.

WSL and Championship clubs recruit regularly at 15 and 16. Some players enter the pathway at 17 or 18, through university football, county pathways, or performance programmes that operate outside the traditional academy structure.

Football at every level, from semi-professional to community, has room for players who develop on their own timeline. The aim is a long, enjoyable relationship with football, whether or not that includes an academy place at 12.

## What Late Developers Often Share

There's no simple profile of a late developer, but players who emerge later often share some qualities:

- **Self-motivation:** players who keep going without outside recognition tend to have internalised their love of the game
- **Resilience:** being overlooked builds a different kind of mental toughness
- **Technical depth:** players who weren't fast-tracked through physical advantage often have more considered technical foundations
- **Character:** not being the obvious talent, and having to work for recognition, often builds qualities that serve a player long-term

These are patterns in players who stayed patient and kept developing.

---

> **Football Parent note:** If your daughter is a late developer, it is hard to watch peers get picked up while she doesn't, and you may wonder whether you should be doing more or whether something has been missed. A more useful question than "why hasn't she been identified yet?" is "is she in the best environment for her right now?" You can answer that one, and act on it. In girls' football, development is only getting going at 13.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What counts as a late developer in girls' football?

It can mean physical late maturation (smaller, less developed physically than peers), technical development that comes on at a different pace, or confidence that takes longer to build. Often these overlap.

### Does puberty really affect football ability?

Yes. The physical changes of puberty, including shifts in body composition, coordination and physical capacity, can temporarily disrupt a player's fluency and athleticism. This is well documented in sports science and is not a sign of declining ability.

### My daughter isn't in a development programme - have we missed the window?

No. The girls' pathway has realistic entry points at 14, 15, and 16, and some routes remain open after that. Not being in a programme at 12 or 13 is not a closed door. See: [What Age Do Girls Football Academies Recruit?](/girls-football/what-age-do-girls-football-academies-recruit)

### How do I support my daughter through a difficult developmental phase?

Keep football enjoyable, find the right environment, and be a calm rather than anxious presence. Separate your concern about pathway from her daily relationship with the game. See: [How To Build Confidence In Young Footballers](/football-development/build-confidence-young-footballers)

### Is grassroots football good enough for a late developer?

Often yes. A high-quality grassroots environment with good coaching, regular game time, and a positive culture can provide exactly what a late developer needs. Put the environment ahead of the level. See: [Girls Academy Football vs Grassroots Football](/girls-football/girls-academy-vs-grassroots-football)

### Can a late developer still reach academy level football?

Yes. Players enter talent pathways at different ages and develop at different rates. While opportunities become more competitive as players get older, late entry into high-level environments is still possible.
