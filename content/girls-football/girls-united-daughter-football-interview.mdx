## Getting Your Daughter Into Football: A Conversation With Girls United

If you are new to girls' football, the questions usually come before the first session. Is there anywhere near us? Will she know anyone? What if she has never played and everyone else has? And once she has started, how do you help without becoming the parent shouting from the touchline?

Football Parent put ten of those questions to **Girls United**, a football organisation that runs girls' clubs and sessions in London and Mexico. The answers came from three of their London team, Lucien Hodgson, Robert Wright and Abigail Ingram, who asked to be credited together as the Girls United team. Their answers are below, in their own words.

## What Has Changed for Girls in the Last Ten Years

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
For parents completely new to girls' football, what opportunities are available today that perhaps didn't exist 5 to 10 years ago?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
The Girls United Team • London
</p>

Many current England players had no choice but to play in boys' teams because there were no all-girls programmes in their area, whereas now girls can find girls-only sessions and teams in far more places, and [participation among women and girls has risen 56% over the last four years](https://www.thefa.com/news/2024/sep/27/women-and-girls-stratgy-progress-inspiring-positive-change-20242709).

Schools have changed too, with [2.6 million girls now having equal access to football in PE](https://www.englandfootball.com/articles/2025/Nov/20/equal-access-to-football-in-pe) after the government pledged in 2023 that girls and boys should be able to play the same sports in lessons and extra-curricular clubs.

## The Less Obvious Barriers

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
What are some of the less obvious barriers that can stop girls getting into football or staying involved?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
The Girls United Team • London
</p>

Cost, transport and parents' work hours can make it hard to get to sessions, and having more venue options closer to where girls actually live would go a long way toward cutting down that travel burden.

Confidence is often the real barrier underneath the practical ones, as some girls feel self-conscious about starting something new or don't have anyone in their family or community who plays.

Others have clashing commitments like church or other activities, and girls with no sporting background can feel like they're already behind before they start.

## Helping a Nervous Girl Love the Game

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
How can parents help their daughters grow a love of the game, especially if they are nervous or do not know many other girls who play?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
The Girls United Team • London
</p>

Take her to watch a local women's team, whether that's professional or your local Sunday league, or stream matches online if getting there isn't possible.

If you're in South London, come to a Girls United Play Like a Girl session, where players feel comfortable even if they've never played or don't know anyone.

Staying positive and encouraging her to be active, rather than putting pressure on results, goes a long way, and if you're not local, find your nearest girls team and try it out, as the game is growing and opportunities are easier to find now.

It's also easy to start living through your daughter's game, and she can feel that.

</p>

## When She's Talented but Lacks Confidence

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
What advice would you give to parents whose daughter is talented but lacks confidence?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
The Girls United Team • London
</p>

Confidence doesn't just build on its own from showing up, so it helps to understand what's actually affecting hers, whether that's a specific skill, a fear of getting things wrong in front of others, or something off the pitch entirely.

Talk to her coach about it, as they can help build a purposeful plan to develop her confidence rather than leaving it to chance. Being deliberate about this makes a real difference, alongside the friendships and familiarity that come from attending regularly.

</p>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">

The players who improve most tend to show real determination, they keep working even when a session is hard or they're not getting it right straight away.

They're positive communicators too, both with coaches and teammates, and that openness makes it easier for them to take feedback on board and actually apply it.

That combination of determination, communication and a genuine willingness to learn is what we see driving the biggest improvements.

</p>

Seeing visible role models, whether that's coaches, older players or professionals, helps her picture herself sticking with the game. Always support her to get to sessions and matches as consistently as possible.

## Final Thoughts

The thread running through every answer is that the first job is getting her there, and keeping her coming back. The practical barriers are real, but Girls United put confidence underneath most of them: the girl who has never played, who doesn't know anyone, or who feels behind before she has started.

For parents, that comes down to a few things you control. Find a girls' session close enough that getting there isn't a weekly battle, and give it time, because familiarity and friendships do a lot of the confidence work. Cheer for the whole team and leave the coaching to the coach, which is one of the [most common football parent mistakes](/parent-guides/biggest-football-parent-mistakes) at any age. If she has ability but holds back, treat it as something to work on with her coach on purpose, alongside the habits in our guide to [building confidence in young footballers](/football-development/build-confidence-young-footballers). And when you are choosing where she plays, the questions in our comparison of [girls' academy and grassroots football](/girls-football/girls-academy-vs-grassroots-football) are a good place to start.

If she later wants to try for a higher level, our guides to [how girls' football academies work](/girls-football/how-girls-football-academies-work) and [girls' football trials](/girls-football/girls-football-trials) explain what comes next. For more from Girls United, follow them on [Instagram at @girlsunitedfa](https://www.instagram.com/girlsunitedfa/).
