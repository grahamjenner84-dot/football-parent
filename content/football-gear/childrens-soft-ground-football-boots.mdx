Plenty of children get through a whole winter in firm ground boots without a problem. The ones who struggle are usually playing 9v9 or 11v11 on heavy grass between November and February, where FG studs fill with mud within minutes and every sharp turn ends in a slip. For them, a pair of children's soft ground football boots helps. For most others, they're an extra pair that rarely gets worn.

The rules add their own complication. Metal studs are allowed on grass in youth football, but 3G pitch rules vary so much that two county FA pitches give opposite answers on whether SG boots are allowed.

## Do Kids Need Soft Ground Football Boots?

Most children don't. A child who trains on 3G and plays matches on a mix of grass and artificial pitches is usually best served by one good pair of FG, multi-ground (MG) or artificial ground (AG) boots, and our [FG vs AG boots guide](/football-gear/ag-vs-fg-boots) explains how to choose between those.

SG boots start to make sense when all three of these are true:

- Your child plays regularly on natural grass through the winter, not just the odd away fixture.
- Those pitches genuinely cut up: standing water, churned goalmouths, mud that clings to the soleplate.
- Your child is slipping in their current boots, especially when turning, pushing off or planting to shoot.

If only one of those applies, an SG pair is likely to spend most of the season in the boot bag. If all three apply, it's a reasonable second pair to add alongside the boots they wear the rest of the time.

## Soft Ground vs Firm Ground Boots: What's Different

The difference is all in the soleplate. Many junior boot models are sold in both FG and SG versions with the same upper and a different soleplate.

**Firm ground (FG) boots** have moulded studs, usually a mix of conical and bladed shapes, built into the soleplate. There are usually around a dozen, of moderate length, spread across the sole to give grip on dry or slightly damp grass.

**Soft ground (SG) boots** have fewer, longer studs, and at least some of them screw in and out. A traditional SG soleplate has six screw-in studs, four under the forefoot and two under the heel. Longer studs push further into soft ground before they find something firm to grip, which is why they hold on mud where FG studs skate across the top.

**Mixed or hybrid soleplates** (sold as SG-Pro, MxSG or "mixed studs") combine a few screw-in studs with moulded studs, often bladed. Many current SG boots, adult and junior, use this layout rather than the traditional six studs.

A few differences parents tend to find out the hard way:

- **Screw-in studs can loosen or fall out.** A boot that's lost a stud mid-match is uneven to stand on, and an exposed thread can be a safety issue for opponents.
- **Replacement studs and a stud key are part of owning them.** SG boots often come with a key; it's the first thing to go missing.

## When SG Boots Help, and When FG Is Fine

Pitch condition is the deciding factor. Plenty of grass pitches drain well and stay firm enough for FG boots through winter; others are waterlogged by October.

**FG boots are usually fine when:**

- The pitch is damp but not cutting up.
- Your child is younger and lighter. A small child tends to sink less into soft ground than a heavier player, and many under-9s play on smaller, less churned pitches in the first place.
- Most of their football is on 3G, with grass only for some matches.

**SG boots tend to help when:**

- The pitch is heavy enough that FG studs fill with mud and clog within minutes.
- Your child plays 9v9 or 11v11 on larger grass pitches, often shared with adult teams on the same site, which take more of a battering.
- They're slipping when changing direction, which is the moment traction matters most and where falls tend to happen.

<ParentNote>

A useful test is to look at your child's boots after a wet match. If the studs are packed solid with mud and the soleplate looks like a flat slab, the boots stopped gripping some time before the final whistle. If the studs are still visible, FG is coping.

## Are Metal Studs Allowed in Youth Football?

Yes, on natural grass, in most youth football in England. There is no age-based ban on metal studs in the Laws of the Game.

Law 4 (The Players' Equipment) doesn't mention studs at all. It says a player must not use or wear anything dangerous, and that all players are inspected before kick-off. In practice, that means the referee or coach checks studs are secure, not sharp or burred, and not worn down to an exposed metal edge. A stud that fails that check has to be changed or removed before the child can play.

The FA's Laws for Mini-Soccer, and county FA guidance for officiating youth football through to 11v11, both put it the same way: correct footwear must be worn for the surface of the pitch, and there are no metal studs on artificial grass pitches. The restriction is tied to the surface, and the guidance sets no age limit.

Where you may still find a ban:

- **Individual leagues or clubs** can set their own footwear rules, particularly for the youngest age groups. Check your league handbook or ask the club secretary before buying.
- **Schools** often have their own policy for school matches and PE.
- **Venues** can refuse metal studs on any surface they own, including grass, if they want to protect it.

In practice, a more common way for a child to be sent to change boots is turning up to a 3G pitch in SG boots.

## Can You Wear Soft Ground Boots on 3G, 4G or Astro?

Usually not, but it depends on the pitch operator. This is where most of the confusion comes from, because two FA-run 3G pitches can have opposite rules.

- **Sussex County FA's 3G pitch** lists SG boots and mixed-stud SG-Pro boots under forbidden footwear, because they tear the surface. Bladed boots are banned too.
- **West Riding County FA's 3G pitch** permits screw-in stud boots, with studs under 21mm, alongside moulded studs, and bans bladed boots instead.
- **Pitch manufacturers** such as TigerTurf publish guidance for their surfaces that says plastic-studded footwear only, no metal studs.

So "can you wear metal studs on 4G?" has no single answer. The safe assumption is no, and the pitch's own footwear sign (usually on the gate or in the changing room) is the final word. Many venues check boots on entry. At West Riding FA's 3G pitch, for example, repeated footwear breaches can get a team's booking cancelled.

<ParentNote>
Most 4G pitches do seem to say no metal studs, as they can tear the artificial grass. They might also give too much grip, which could lead to a serious injury if your foot gets caught. Most suggest moulded studs for 4G, as they give you good grip without the same risk.
</ParentNote>

Beyond the rules, SG boots are a poor choice on artificial turf anyway. Long studs catch in the rubber crumb and carpet rather than releasing cleanly, which is the same pressure and grip problem our [FG vs AG boots guide](/football-gear/ag-vs-fg-boots) covers for FG studs, only more pronounced. Astro (older sand-based and short-pile pitches) is worse still: SG studs have nothing to sink into at all.

If your child trains on 3G midweek and plays on muddy grass at the weekend, the realistic answer is two pairs: AG for 3G training, SG only for grass matches. Buying the same model in both soleplates keeps the fit consistent between the two.

What to look for in a junior SG boot:

## Buying and Looking After Junior SG Boots

A few habits make SG boots last longer and keep them within the rules:

- **Check every stud before each match.** Tighten any loose ones with the key. Stripped threads mean the boot needs a new stud, or eventually a new pair.
- **Replace worn studs early.** Metal studs wear down on car parks, paths and changing-room floors, and a worn stud can develop a sharp edge that fails a referee's check.
- **Don't wear them off the pitch.** Walking to the pitch on tarmac in SG boots wears studs fastest and is the easiest way to slip.
- **Clean the mud off before it dries.** Dried mud around screw-in studs makes them harder to remove and replace.
- **Buy the size that fits now.** SG boots only get worn for part of the year, so a pair bought a size too big "to last" may never be worn at the right size.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I wear SG boots on firm ground?

You can, but it's not a good idea for long. On a hard, dry pitch the long studs can't sink in, so your child ends up balancing on a few points. It's uncomfortable and gives less stable grip than FG studs would. Keep SG boots for genuinely soft days.

### Can you turn SG boots into FG boots?

Not really. You can swap metal studs for shorter or plastic screw-in studs, which makes them a little more usable on firmer ground, but the soleplate still has the SG layout with fewer, widely spaced studs. It won't behave like an FG boot, and it won't make it acceptable on a 3G pitch that bans SG soleplates.

### What studs are illegal in football?

No stud type is banned outright by the Laws of the Game. A stud becomes illegal when it's dangerous: sharp, burred, worn to an exposed edge or loose. Separately, many 3G venues ban metal studs, screw-in studs or bladed boots on their surface, and some leagues or clubs set their own footwear rules.

### Do junior SG boots come with studs and a key?

Many do, with the studs already fitted, but check the listing. Buy a spare set of studs that fits the specific model at the same time, because a lost stud on a matchday morning is much easier to deal with if you have one in the kit bag.

### Are moulded boots OK on soft ground?

For young children on smaller pitches, often yes. Moulded or FG boots cope with most damp grass. They struggle on genuinely heavy, muddy pitches, where the studs clog and stop gripping.
