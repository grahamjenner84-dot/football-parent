A football tracker for a child is one of three things: a GPS pod that sits in a vest between the shoulder blades, a sensor strapped to the calf, or a sensor strapped to the boot. They measure different things, they cost very different amounts to keep running once any subscription kicks in, and the numbers mean far less at under-9 than they do at under-15.

The short answer: STATSports Academy is the GPS vest with no subscription to pay, the Footbar Meteor is the cheapest way in and works indoors, and CityPlay is the pick for a child who cares more about touches and their weaker foot than distance and top speed.

## Three Kinds of Football Tracker

**GPS vest.** A small pod slides into a pocket on a snug vest worn under the shirt, the same set-up you see on professionals in training. It tracks movement across the pitch: distance, top speed, number of sprints, accelerations and a heatmap of where your child played. It knows nothing about the ball. GPS also needs a clear view of the sky, so a vest is built for outdoor grass and 3G pitches, not a sports hall.

**Calf sensor.** The Footbar Meteor straps around the lower leg and reads leg movement rather than satellite position. That lets it estimate shots, passes and shot power as well as distance and sprint speed, and it carries on working for futsal and indoor five-a-side.

**Boot sensor.** Playermaker and CityPlay strap a sensor to each boot. Because there is one on each foot, they are the only type that can tell you how often your child uses their left foot against their right, how many touches they took and how they released the ball.

The simplest way to choose: if the question in your house is "how fast am I?", a vest answers it. If it is "am I actually using my left foot?", only a boot sensor can.

## Best Football GPS Trackers for Kids

Each pick from the card above in more detail, including the trade-off that comes with it.

### STATSports Academy: the GPS vest with nothing more to pay

STATSports supplies GPS systems to professional clubs, and the Academy vest is its version for individual players. It records 24 metrics, including top speed, distance, sprints and intensity, and the app adds drills and training plans built from your child's own data, plus masterclass videos from England players.

The reason it tops this list for most families is that it is a one-off purchase: the listing is explicit that no subscription is required. It costs the most upfront, so it makes most sense for a child playing 11-a-side who will wear it for several seasons. When they outgrow the vest, STATSports sells the vest on its own, so you are not buying the pod twice.

### PitcheroGPS: a cheaper GPS vest with a yearly fee

PitcheroGPS comes from the same UK company behind the Pitchero club websites many grassroots clubs already use. The pod records 18 metrics, including high-speed running, accelerations, decelerations and impacts as well as distance and top speed, and it is sold in youth sizes that fit younger players better than an adult small.

The trade-off is the app. Sixty days of access come in the box, and after that a yearly subscription is needed to keep seeing the data. It is cheaper than STATSports to buy, but over two or three seasons the subscription can close that gap, so do the sum before choosing on the box price.

### Footbar Meteor: the budget pick, and it works indoors

The Meteor is the cheapest tracker here by a distance and has by far the most Amazon reviews of anything on this list. It straps to the calf with an adjustable elastic band that fits any size of leg, which matters for a child who will grow out of a sized vest within a season or two. The battery lasts around four days, and your phone does not need to be near the pitch while it records.

Because it reads leg movement rather than GPS, it works for futsal, indoor five-a-side and sports halls, where a GPS vest loses its signal. The core stats are free in the app, with an optional subscription for extras such as setting targets and logging goals and assists.

### CityPlay: a boot sensor for touches and the weaker foot

CityPlay runs on Playermaker's boot sensors and is aimed at younger players, with Manchester City coaching content and practice suggestions built into the app. It reports over 25 football-specific metrics, including two-footed play, first touch, dribbling and where on the foot the ball was released.

This is the one to pick for a child working on their weaker foot or their first touch, because a vest cannot see either. The sensors sit on straps around the boot, sold in medium and large, so check the strap size against your child's boots before ordering.

### Also worth knowing: Playermaker 2.0 and SoccerBee

Playermaker 2.0 is the same boot-sensor system without the Manchester City content, and its app membership renews automatically every 12 months. SoccerBee's Bee Pro 2 is a GPS vest with a heatmap, sprint direction and comparisons against players in the same age group, but it needs a subscription and its listing prices that in US dollars, so it is built for the American market first.

### Progress: for goals, assists and minutes

A tracker records movement. [Progress](/progress) is our app for the match itself: log their goals, assists, minutes and where they played, and it builds a record across every season. It works alongside any of the trackers above, with nothing to wear.

## Which Football Trackers Need a Subscription?

This is where the real cost of a tracker sits, and it is the detail most listings put furthest down the page.

- **STATSports Academy:** no subscription. One-off purchase, app included.
- **Footbar Meteor:** core stats free. Optional subscription for extra features.
- **PitcheroGPS:** 60 days of app access included, then a yearly subscription.
- **SoccerBee Bee Pro 2:** subscription required, with a 30-day free trial.
- **Playermaker 2.0:** app membership included for 12 months, then renews automatically.
- **CityPlay:** check the current terms on the listing before buying.

The common misconception is that the price on the box is the price of the tracker. For a child who will use one for three seasons, add up the box and three years of the app before comparing. Set a reminder for any auto-renewal date, too: a tracker that ends up in a kit bag drawer by February can keep charging well after it stopped being worn.

## Is Your Child Old Enough for a Tracker?

There is no minimum age on most of these, and vests come in youth sizes, so the real question is whether the numbers will mean anything yet.

On the small pitches used for younger age groups, distance and top speed are small and swing with pitch size, position and how many players are on the field. A child who covers more ground in their first 9v9 season than they did at 7v7 has not necessarily got fitter: the pitch got bigger. Our guide to [the FA's youth football formats](/football-development/new-fa-youth-football-format) sets out how pitch sizes change through the age groups.

Sprint speed in particular tracks physical maturity more than footballing ability around puberty. An early developer, or a September birthday in a team of summer-borns, will often top a speed chart without being the better player, and the birth-month side of that is the [relative age effect in football](/football-development/relative-age-effect-football). Comparing distance or touches with teammates can be genuinely useful, but a sprint-speed league table can leave a late developer feeling slow when the gap is down to growth.

Another misconception is that more distance means a better game. A centre-back who reads danger early can cover less ground than one who is constantly chasing back, and a winger who has drifted out of the team's shape can rack up distance doing very little. Distance on its own says nothing about the quality of a performance.

Trackers tend to make more sense around the move to 11-a-side, for a child who wants to train on their own and is curious about their numbers. If they are doing extra running on top of club training, a tracker can also help you see when a week has been [too much training](/football-development/how-much-training-is-too-much).

## Wearing a GPS Vest in Matches

Tell the coach before your child wears a tracker in a league match. Referees can check any equipment a player wears, and a coach would rather know in advance than have it questioned at kick-off. Several of the vests above carry a "FIFA approved" line, which refers to FIFA's quality programme for player tracking devices.

<ParentNote>
As a coach, I wouldn't have a problem with a player wearing one. I'd probably be interested to see what numbers they had and what it measured.
</ParentNote>

A vest should sit as snugly as a compression top, because a pod that bounces around in a loose pocket gives worse data and annoys the child wearing it. Take the pod out before washing the vest, and charge it the night before a match rather than on the morning, when it will be forgotten in the rush for boots and shin pads.

A simple matchday routine: pod charged and in the vest the night before, tracker switched on in the warm-up, synced in the car afterwards, and the numbers left alone until your child asks about them.

## What to Do With the Numbers

A single match on its own tells you very little. The numbers start to mean something once there is something to compare them with: the same child across a run of matches, or a teammate wearing the same tracker. Top speed rising across a year, or more touches with the weaker foot after a month of practice, is worth noticing. One low-distance game is not.

<ParentNote>
For me it's more interesting when you have something to compare it to, either a friend's numbers or a number of matches, to get an idea of how things have gone and what is out of kilter. The things I'd find interesting are the distance they ran, touches, and being able to see which foot they used the most. Again, that's most interesting compared to the team average.
</ParentNote>

Let your child lead. If they want to look at their sprint speed after a match, look with them. If they don't, the car journey home is not the place to bring it up: our guide on [what to say after a football match](/parent-guides/what-to-say-after-football-matches) covers why that conversation goes better without performance talk.

A tracker will not help a child get scouted either. Academy recruitment runs on scouts and coaches watching players in games and trials, as our guide to [how football scouts identify players](/academy-trials/how-football-scouts-identify-players) explains, and numbers from a consumer app are not part of that process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do professional footballers wear GPS trackers?

Yes. The vest you can see under a professional's shirt in training, and often in matches, holds a GPS pod. Clubs' sports science staff use the data to manage how hard each player is working across a week, which is a very different job to a parent checking one match.

### What is the best football tracker for kids?

For most children starting out, the Footbar Meteor, because it is the cheapest, fits any size of leg and works indoors. For a child playing 11-a-side who wants GPS data, STATSports Academy, because there is no subscription. For a child working on their weaker foot or first touch, CityPlay.

### Do football GPS trackers work indoors?

GPS vests do not, because GPS needs a clear view of the sky. Motion-based trackers such as the Footbar Meteor and the Playermaker and CityPlay boot sensors do, so they work for futsal and indoor five-a-side.

### Is there a football GPS tracker with no subscription?

STATSports Academy is a one-off purchase with its app included. The Footbar Meteor is not GPS, but its core stats are free, with an optional paid tier for extras.
