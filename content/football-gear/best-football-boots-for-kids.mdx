Buying football boots for a child rarely stays simple. There are three or four sole types to choose between, feet that need checking for size every few months, and a price range that has surprisingly little to do with which boot is actually right for a seven-year-old. This guide covers what actually matters before you buy: sole type, fit, budget, and how those priorities shift as your child gets older.

## Boot Types Explained: FG, AG, SG and Moulded

Most children's football boots fall into one of four categories, and the label is usually printed on the box and on the soleplate itself.

**FG (Firm Ground)** boots are built for natural grass, with fewer, longer conical or bladed studs. They're what most people picture when they think of a football boot.

**AG (Artificial Ground)** boots are built for 3G and 4G artificial pitches, with more studs that are shorter and more widely spread. A large share of grassroots training, and a growing amount of junior match football, now happens on 3G rather than grass, which makes this the more relevant category for many families. The full reasoning, including why the wrong choice here can cause real discomfort, is covered in our [FG vs AG boots guide](/football-gear/ag-vs-fg-boots).

**SG (Soft Ground)** boots use metal or hard plastic screw-in studs, designed for soft, wet natural grass in winter conditions. Law 4 of the Laws of the Game requires that a player's equipment must not be dangerous, and referees check footwear as part of the pre-match inspection, but [the FA's published Law 4 text](https://www.thefa.com/football-rules-governance/lawsandrules/laws/football-11-11/law-4---the-players-equipment) doesn't set out stud specifics beyond that general safety requirement. In practice, most 3G and 4G pitch operators simply don't allow metal or hard screw-in studs on their surfaces, so SG boots are a natural-grass-only option.

**Moulded boots** (sometimes called turf trainers or TF boots) have many small rubber studs and work reasonably well across hard ground and firmer artificial surfaces. For young children, they're often the simplest and most durable choice.

## Which Sole Type Does Your Child Need?

Start with where your child actually plays, not with what looks most like a "proper" football boot.

- **Mostly 3G training and matches:** AG boots.
- **Mostly dry natural grass:** FG boots.
- **Mostly soft, muddy natural grass in winter:** SG boots, once your child is old enough and their club permits screw-in studs.
- **A genuine mix, or you're not sure:** AG boots are the safer default, since wearing FG or SG studs regularly on artificial turf creates the pressure-point problems covered in our FG vs AG guide.
- **Under-7s and mini-soccer:** moulded boots are a practical, low-maintenance option regardless of surface.

<ParentNote>
My son would sometimes wear his astro boots on a 3G pitch, and it wasn't a major issue. But one coach at a higher-level session, focused heavily on ball mastery and 1v1s with a lot of cutting and changing direction, suggested AG or moulded boots would help on that surface. He did say my son was a bit of a perfectionist, so I wouldn't call it essential, just something that could help.
</ParentNote>

That's a useful example of the gap between "fine" and "ideal": the wrong sole type on the wrong surface isn't usually going to cause an obvious problem, but it can cost a young player a bit of grip and stability in exactly the moments, sharp turns and quick direction changes, where that matters most.

## Fit Matters More Than Brand

No boot brand makes feet grow correctly, and a well-fitting mid-range boot will always serve a child better than an ill-fitting premium one. Good fit means roughly a thumb's width of room at the toe, no pinching at the sides, and a snug but not tight fit around the heel. If your child has noticeably wider feet, our [wide feet buying guide](/football-gear/boots/best-football-boots-for-wide-feet-kids) goes into the brands and models that tend to accommodate width better.

Fastening matters too, and it's easy to overlook. Younger children who can't yet lace their own boots reliably need help before every single session, which sounds minor until it's happening several times a week.

My son insisted on a pair with an internal sock, but at seven he needed help getting his foot in every time, which became a real pain before every session. Before that he'd had a velcro pair that were easy to slip on and off himself.
</ParentNote>

## Budget vs Premium: What's Worth Paying For

At grassroots level, for a child whose feet are still growing quickly, premium boots rarely justify their price difference. The performance benefits of top-tier boots, lighter materials, marginal grip improvements, are built for players whose technique and physical development can actually make use of them.

<ParentNote>
He's probably had two pairs a season, partly because his feet have grown quickly and partly because he started playing five times a week, so they got heavily used and started to show wear and tear. We usually buy decent brands, so I don't think it's particularly down to quality. I'd suggest at the younger ages it's best not buying the most expensive ones if they're growing quickly.
</ParentNote>

That's a useful distinction: replacement is often driven as much by how often a child plays as by growth alone. A boot that would comfortably last a season for a child training once a week may not survive a season for a child training and playing five times a week, regardless of price point.

## Boots by Age and Stage

Boot priorities shift as children move through the FA's youth football formats, covered in full in our [guide to the new FA youth football format](/football-development/new-fa-youth-football-format).

**3v3 and 5v5 stages (Under-7 to Under-9):** comfort and independence matter most. Moulded or AG boots that are easy to get on and off, without an expensive spec your child won't notice.

**7v7 and 9v9 stages (Under-10 to Under-13):** boots start to see more consistent use as training frequency increases. This is usually the stage where replacement frequency, not sole type, becomes the main budget question.

**11v11 (Under-14 and up):** as children play a more adult-sized game and their feet grow more slowly, it becomes more reasonable to invest in a boot that will actually last a full season, and sole type choice starts to matter more for performance as well as comfort.

## How Often to Replace Boots

Check fit every few months rather than only at the start of a season. The [Royal College of Podiatry's children's footwear guidance](https://rcpod.org.uk/api/documentlibrary/download?documentId=469) recommends checking children's footwear roughly every three months from around age four, more often during a growth spurt, since feet can outgrow boots well before they look obviously worn out.

Alongside growth, watch for wear-based signs it's time to replace a pair regardless of size: studs worn down unevenly, the sole starting to separate from the upper, or stitching coming loose around the toe. A boot that's structurally breaking down won't protect or support a foot the way a new one does, even if it technically still fits.

## Common Buying Mistakes

**Assuming a more expensive boot is automatically better for a young player.** For a growing foot, fit and appropriate sole type matter far more than premium materials or marginal performance gains.

**Treating FG as the "default" boot without checking the surface.** Many parents buy FG because it looks like a proper football boot, without checking whether their child actually trains and plays mostly on 3G, where AG is the more appropriate choice.

**Sizing up to fix a width problem.** A boot that's too long to compensate for narrow width causes the foot to slide inside the boot, which creates its own problems with control and heel rubbing. Width and length need separate solutions, covered in more detail in our [wide feet guide](/football-gear/boots/best-football-boots-for-wide-feet-kids).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What age should my child move from moulded boots to a sport-specific sole?

There's no fixed age. Many children stay in moulded boots comfortably through mini-soccer, and move to AG or FG once they're training more regularly on a consistent surface, which often lines up with the move into 7v7 or 9v9 football.

### Can my child wear SG boots for grassroots football?

Some children do, on natural grass in wet winter conditions, but not every club or pitch allows screw-in studs at every age, and they're never appropriate on 3G or 4G surfaces. Check with your child's coach or club before buying a pair.

### Do more expensive boots help a young player improve?

Not meaningfully at grassroots level. Fit, comfort and the right sole type for the surface make a bigger practical difference than premium materials, which are built for players whose technique can make use of marginal performance gains.

### Should I buy boots online or try them in a shop?

Try in a shop where possible, wearing the football socks your child will actually play in. If you do buy online, use a retailer with straightforward returns, since sizing varies noticeably between brands.

### How do I know if my child has outgrown their boots?

Check for less than a thumb's width of room at the toe, red marks or indentations on the foot after wearing them, or a boot that looks visibly stretched across the toe box. Checking every few months, not just at the start of a season, catches this before it becomes uncomfortable.
