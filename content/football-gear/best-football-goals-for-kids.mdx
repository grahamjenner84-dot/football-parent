Football goals for the garden are one of the few bits of football kit that get used every single day, not just on matchday. But "kids football goals" covers everything from a £15 pop-up net to a match-standard 16ft frame, and the size, type and how you anchor it all matter more than most parents expect before they buy one.

## What Size Football Goal Does My Child Need?

England Football's [FutureFit reforms](https://futurefit.englandfootball.com/futurefit/faqs/what-are-pitch-and-goal-sizes-for-the-different-age-groups/index.html), which we've covered in full in our guide to the [new FA youth football format](/football-development/new-fa-youth-football-format), set out the goal size that goes with each playing format from the 2026/27 season:

- **3v3 (Under 7s):** around 4ft x 2.5ft
- **5v5 (Under 8s and Under 9s):** 12ft x 6ft
- **7v7 (Under 10s and Under 11s):** 12ft x 6ft
- **9v9 (Under 12s and Under 13s):** 16ft x 7ft
- **11v11 (Under 14s):** 21ft x 7ft
- **11v11 (Under 15s and up):** full-size, 24ft x 8ft

For actual matches, your club will already have the right size goal, so none of this affects what your child plays on a Saturday. It's mainly useful as a reference point for garden practice: matching the goal at home to roughly the goal they shoot at in matches means the height and width of a save or a finish feels familiar, rather than shooting at a target that's a different shape to the one they'll face at the weekend.

In practice, most gardens can't fit a genuine match-size goal for any format past 5v5, so the realistic range for home use runs from a mini goal (around 4ft x 2.5ft, good for very young children or 1v1 games) through a 6ft x 4ft general-purpose size, up to an 8ft x 5ft goal for older, secondary-school-age children who are already shooting and volleying with real power. An 8ft x 5ft goal is still well short of the 12ft x 6ft goals used for 5v5 and 7v7 and the 16ft x 7ft goal used for 9v9, all of which are bigger than most UK gardens can realistically take, but it's a proper step up from a 6ft x 4ft frame once a child has outgrown it.

## Pop-Up Goals vs Folding Goals

Two different designs turn up under "kids football goals", and they suit different situations.

**Pop-up goals** spring open when you release a strap, and fold flat again by twisting the frame back into a circle. They're the fastest option to get set up, which matters if you've genuinely only got 20 or 30 minutes before tea, but the frame is a lighter fibreglass pole rather than steel, so they're better suited to shooting practice than to being climbed on or used as a full-time fixture.

**Folding goals** (sometimes called "instant" or "quick-fold" goals) use a steel frame that unfolds and locks into place. Set-up takes a bit longer than a pop-up, but the frame is sturdier and closer to what your child will actually shoot at during a match.

## Anchoring a Goal Safely

This is the one part of buying a goal that's genuinely a safety issue, not just a buying preference. Most garden-sized goals fall under the British Standard PAS 36 rather than the European standard EN 748, which only covers full-size goals and the 5m-wide youth size, but the [FA's own goalpost safety guidance](https://www.thefa.com/GetIntoFootball/Players/~/media/Files/PDF/Get%20into%20Football/GoalpostSafety_GuidanceNotes_Dec05.ashx) is direct on this point regardless of standard: it applies to goals "of all types and sizes", and goals of any size, including small, portable ones bought for home use, should always be anchored securely to the ground or weighted so they can't tip forward. A lightweight frame with a child hanging off the crossbar, or caught by a gust of wind, is a real tipping risk, not a theoretical one.

Most garden goals come with ground stakes, which are fine on grass but do nothing on a patio or hard standing, and won't help much in genuinely windy weather even on a lawn. A sandbag or weighted base, sold with some goals and rebounders, is the more reliable option if the goal is staying out for any length of time.

<ParentNote>
We sometimes take out the washing line pole, since it's in the middle of the garden anyway, and rest it against the back of the goal if it's windy. We also bought a rebounder that came with a sandbag, which is probably the more official way of doing it.
</ParentNote>

Whatever you use, the goal should not be able to tip forward under its own weight if a child pulls down on the crossbar or catches it while going for the ball. Check this once it's actually anchored, rather than assuming the stakes or weight that came in the box are automatically enough for your garden.

## Nets, Storage and What Wears Out First

The frame is rarely what fails first on a garden goal. It's usually the net.

<ParentNote>
Our original mistake was buying a smaller goal and leaving the net up all the time. Foxes, or something else in the garden, made holes in it. It was subtle at first, but eventually the net was basically non-existent. Since then we've taken the net down after each use, and bought a target net separately, which is quicker to put up if we've only got half an hour or so, and good for aiming at the corners rather than just shooting to beat a goalkeeper.
</ParentNote>

Taking a net down after every session is more effort than leaving it up, but it's the difference between a net that lasts a season and one that needs replacing within a few weeks, the same "fit for now, not for next year" logic that applies to [choosing the right ball size](/football-gear/best-footballs-by-age). If your child mainly wants to practise aiming rather than beating a full goal, a smaller target net that goes up in seconds is worth having alongside a full-size goal rather than instead of it.

## One Big Goal or Two Small Ones?

It's tempting to buy the biggest goal you can fit, on the logic that a bigger target now means less to outgrow later. Whether that's the right call depends on what you actually want the goal for.

<ParentNote>
We went from a smaller goal to a bigger one because I thought he'd benefit from hitting shots higher against a keeper, since the smaller goal had him used to shooting low and hard. That did lead to more shooting and goalkeeping practice between us. But looking back, if I had that younger age again, I'd buy two of the smaller 1v1-sized goals instead and set up loads of small matches. It's such a good way to get lots of touches on the ball under pressure and get used to beating a player in a tight space, rather than just shooting at an open goal.
</ParentNote>

Both approaches have a genuine place. A single bigger goal is better for shooting and goalkeeping practice specifically, because it forces a child to place the ball rather than just get it past a smaller target. Two small goals, one at each end of the garden with a keeper or a defender in between, turns the same space into a proper small-sided game, closer to what your child is actually being asked to do more of under the FA's newer 3v3 and 5v5 formats. It's also a good way to work on the split-second choices covered in our guide to [improving football decision-making](/football-development/improve-football-decision-making), and the kind of repeated pressure situation that helps [build confidence in young footballers](/football-development/build-confidence-young-footballers).

If space allows, the cheapest way to get both is a single mid-size goal plus one mini goal, rather than doubling up on the same size twice.

## Buying for Christmas or a Birthday

Football goals are a genuinely popular gift, whether that's a main present on its own or one part of a wider kit refresh alongside [new boots](/football-gear/best-football-boots-for-kids) for the new season, or something to go along with a kids' football-themed party. A few things are worth knowing before you order one as a present rather than for immediate use:

- Check the actual footprint fits where it's going to live, not just where it'll be unwrapped. A 6ft x 4ft goal needs more clearance behind it than the box suggests, both for the goal itself and for a child taking a run-up.
- Steel-framed folding goals take longer to assemble than pop-up ones. If it's a surprise present that needs to be ready to use straight away, factor in assembly time before the big day, not on it.
- Order earlier than you think you need to. Goals are seasonal stock, and popular sizes can sell out in the run-up to Christmas in particular.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between QuickPlay, Forza and other football goal brands?

Forza is another well-known UK brand people search alongside "football goals", and it makes a similar range of pop-up and folding goals. The things actually worth comparing before you buy from any of them are the accuracy of the stated size against the format your child plays, the frame material (steel folding frames generally outlast fibreglass pop-up poles under regular use), and what the warranty and after-sales support actually cover if a part fails.

### How much does a decent football goal cost?

A mini goal for the youngest children starts at around £30-£35. A general-purpose 6ft x 4ft goal for primary-age children is typically £50-£70, though a sturdier locking-frame design can run to around £120. A dedicated pop-up "instant" goal is typically £120-£190. You don't need to spend at the top of any of these ranges for a goal that gets used a handful of times a week in a normal-sized garden.

### Is a football goal a good present for a kids' football party?

A mini goal or two, plus a target net, works well for a football-themed party because several children can use it at once without waiting for a full-size goal to be free. A single larger goal is better suited to one-on-one shooting practice than to a group of children at a party.
