At 7 and 8, most children don't need a coaching qualification in the house, a full-size goal or a structured programme to get better with a ball at their feet. They need a few minutes, a bit of space, and someone willing to throw a ball at them or try to nick it off them. This guide covers the drills that actually work at this age, how long a session realistically needs to be, and a few things that are easy to get wrong.

## Why 7 and 8 Is a Good Age for Ball Mastery

England Football's own player development framework treats ages 5-11 as a distinct "Foundation Phase", with separate guidance for the 5-8 and 9-12 bands because what a child needs at each stage differs. For the Foundation Phase, the FA's stated priority is straightforward: [players should "stay on the ball, master the ball" and "develop a mastery of the ball and the confidence to try new things"](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/england-dna/the-future-england-player/age-phase-priorities), rather than being drilled on shape, position or tactics.

That lines up with what small-sided formats at this age are already built for. From the 2026/27 season, U7s play 3v3 and U8s play 5v5 under the FA's [FutureFit reforms](/football-development/new-fa-youth-football-format), both deliberately small formats that mean more individual touches and more one-on-one moments per game than an older, larger-format game gives a child. Practising ball mastery at home is essentially rehearsing for the exact situations a 7 or 8 year old already faces most often in a real match.

None of this means a child who doesn't get extra practice at home is behind. It just means that if a parent wants to do something useful with 10 spare minutes, ball mastery is a good use of it at this age, more useful than shape or team tactics, which the Foundation Phase approach deliberately leaves until later. That changes once a child moves up to 7-a-side at Under 10: our guide to [7-a-side and 9-a-side formations](/coaching/best-football-formations-by-age-group) covers the shapes coaches actually use from that age.

## Garden and Small-Space Drills You Can Do Together

You don't need much room. A line of cones (or jumpers, or anything that won't roll) in a garden, a driveway or even a hallway is enough for most of this.

<ParentNote>
When he was 6-7, we'd set up a line of cones in the garden. He'd dribble through, do a one-two with me, then dribble through the next set doing something different, foot rolls for example, before finishing with a shot at goal. He always wanted to start with me throwing the ball high in the air for him to control. Giving him a fun bit at the end and a bit of control over the session, like that big throw to start, kept it fun for him. We'd mix it up with different challenges too: left foot only, right foot only, dribbling backwards through the cones.
</ParentNote>

A few things make this kind of circuit work better than it sounds:

- **Bookend it with something fun.** A shot at the end, or a save they get to make, gives the whole circuit a payoff rather than feeling like a set of instructions.
- **Give them a small choice.** Letting your child pick how the session starts, or which challenge to do next, makes it feel like their session rather than yours.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Left foot only, then right foot only, then dribbling backwards through the cones, keeps the same simple circuit from going stale without you having to invent something new every session.

## Dribbling Drills and Skill Combinations

Basic dribbling through cones covers close control in a straight line. Skill combinations, stringing two or three moves together, cover something different: agility, balance and the ability to change direction quickly.

<ParentNote>
We'd also have a ball out in the house whenever we could, little passes, kicking it against a wall when his mum allowed it. Just getting more touches, on both feet, makes a difference. The thing I wish I'd done more of early on is skill combinations. I used to see people practising things like a step-over into a Maradona turn into an L-drag and think it was a waste of time, not something you'd actually do in a match. But it builds real agility, balance and foot speed, and that carries over into ball mastery generally.
</ParentNote>

A simple way to introduce this without it feeling technical:

- Pick two moves your child already half-knows (a step-over and a drag-back is a good starting pair) and get them to link the two, slowly at first, over the same cone.
- Don't worry about whether the exact combination would happen in a match. The footwork and balance a child builds linking moves together is what actually carries over.
- Let your child name their own moves. It sounds trivial, but a "spin turn" they've named themselves gets practised far more willingly than one you've instructed them to do.

## 1v1 Games That Build Close Control

If you only have time for one thing, this is the one worth prioritising.

<ParentNote>
The thing that had the biggest impact in hindsight was 1v1 against me. We'd each defend a goal and just dribble and tackle each other. It really helped his close control and shielding. Looking back, I'd have set one rule sooner: no turning your back on the goal. For a while, as soon as I put him under pressure he'd spin away and shield the ball instead of trying to get past me. Shielding's a fair skill to have, but in a real game there's usually a second defender closing in while you're still shielding the first one, so it only buys a few seconds before you lose the ball anyway. I wish I'd pushed him to try beating his man more often, and only shield once a second defender was actually closing in.
</ParentNote>

To set this up: mark out two small goals a few metres apart (cones, shoes, whatever's around), and take turns attacking and defending. A few adjustments worth making as your child gets more comfortable:

- Once shielding stops being a challenge for them, add a simple rule: no shielding until an actual second defender is closing in. That keeps shielding available for genuinely tight moments without it becoming the default response to any pressure.
- Let them win sometimes without obviously letting them win. Genuine 1v1 games at this age work best when the outcome isn't fixed either way.
- Keep it short. A few minutes of proper 1v1 is more useful than 20 minutes where both of you have lost focus.

## Simple Passing Drills for Two

Passing needs a partner, but it doesn't need much else. A wall works if a second person isn't available.

- **Two-touch passing.** Stand a few metres apart and pass with a first touch to control, second touch to pass. Move a step further apart every minute or two.
- **Weaker-foot only.** The same passing drill, but only using the foot your child avoids. It's easy to let this slide because it's less rewarding than a shot at goal, but a few minutes most sessions is enough to stop a habit forming.
- **Wall passing.** If you're on your own with your child, or need a break, a wall or a garage door lets them keep passing and controlling without a second player.
- **Little challenges.** I remember for a while we had a thing when we went to the park where we had to do 50 one touch passes in a row on the path without the ball touching the grass. Just stupid but fun games we made up that meant more touches.

Combining passing with a bit of movement, a short jog to receive rather than standing still, makes it slightly more game-realistic without turning a simple drill into something complicated.

## How Long a Session Should Be

There's a common assumption that more time practising means more improvement. At 7 and 8, that's not really how it plays out.

<ParentNote>
We used to aim for about 10 minutes and set a stopwatch. We'd agree he couldn't end on a bad touch, and I'd often pretend he was about to break his own record to keep him motivated. To be honest, at 6-7 he was keen to do almost anything, so holding his attention wasn't too difficult.
</ParentNote>

A focused 10 minutes most days will do more for a 7 or 8 year old's ball mastery than an occasional 45-minute session that both of you are trying to get through. [How much training is genuinely useful at different ages](/football-development/how-much-training-is-too-much) covers this in more depth if you're weighing up home practice alongside club training and other sports. The main thing to watch for isn't the clock, it's whether your child still wants to be out there. The moment it becomes a chore rather than a game, that session's done its job for the day.

If home practice on its own doesn't feel like enough structure, [private or small-group coaching](/football-development/is-private-football-coaching-worth-it) is worth reading about as a separate option, rather than something to add on top of daily home sessions.

## A Simple Drill List to Work From

If you want something to glance at before heading into the garden rather than working it out from scratch each time, here's a short list built from the drills above:

1. Cone dribble, one-two, second dribble variation, shot at goal.
2. Same circuit, left foot only.
3. Same circuit, right foot only.
4. Same circuit, dribbling backwards through the cones.
5. One skill combination, practised slowly over a single cone.
6. Two-touch passing, moving further apart every minute.
7. Weaker-foot-only passing.
8. 1v1, small goals each, staying forward rather than shielding.

Ten minutes covers three or four of these on a given day. There's no need to get through the whole list every session, rotating a couple of them keeps it fresh.

Whatever your child enjoys most in this list is worth doing more of, even if it isn't the one you'd have picked. [What actually makes a good development environment](/football-development/good-football-development-environment) applies just as much in the garden as it does at a club: enjoyment and repetition tend to matter more than how structured a session looks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What football drills are best for a 7 year old?

A simple cone circuit finishing in a shot, basic two-touch passing, and short 1v1 games cover most of what's useful at this age. Keep instructions to one thing at a time rather than layering up coaching points.

### What football drills are best for an 8 year old?

The same core drills work, but an 8 year old can usually handle slightly longer sessions and a bit more structure, like linking two skill moves together rather than practising them separately.

### How long should a drill session be for a 7 or 8 year old?

Many families find 10 to 15 minutes of focused practice is enough, especially if it's most days rather than one long session a week. Let your child's attention, not the clock, decide when to stop.

### Do I need any equipment, or can we do this with just a ball?

A ball is the only essential. English youth football uses a size 3 ball up to Under 11, so whatever size your child already uses at training is the right one for these drills too, there's no need to buy anything bigger. [The full breakdown of football sizes by age](/football-gear/best-footballs-by-age) covers it if you're not sure. Beyond the ball, everything else in this guide uses things most families already have around the house.

### Is it worth practising with your child's weaker foot at this age?

Yes! A heavily one-footed player is genuinely less effective running with the ball: a good opponent will show them onto their weaker side and back themselves to win it there, knowing the child can't beat them on it. The other giveaway is in the first touch, not just running with it. Watch closely during a game and you'll see some children control the ball back across their body onto their stronger foot even when it arrives on the "wrong" side. That costs them time on the next touch, and it cuts down the passing and shooting angles actually open to them, because the ball's already travelled across their body instead of staying out in front.

Simply repeating the same drills weaker-foot-only, without making it a big deal, tends to work better than singling it out as a separate "problem" to fix. To help my son shoot left footed we had a 'rule' he'd do ten every day for a while. Within a week he was already much more confident and at 7 and 8 you'll be amazed how quickly they pick things up.
