The best drills for 9 and 10 year olds are about receiving the ball, turning with it and choosing between a dribble and a pass, because that is what the step up to 7-a-side asks of them. Ball mastery still matters, but at this age it needs to happen with a defender nearby and a decision to make. This guide covers the drills that work for a team session or the garden, how to string them into a session, and the habits that are easy to coach out by accident.

## What Changes at 9 and 10

Under the FA's FutureFit formats, Under 10s and Under 11s play 7v7, so most 9 and 10 year olds are either in their first 7-a-side season or their second. [England Football's 7v7 guidance](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles-and-resources/coaching/resources/2025/How-to-play-and-manage-7v7-games) sets a recommended pitch of 55 x 37 metres, a size 3 ball and matches of up to 50 minutes, with a retreat line and no offside. Our guide to [7-a-side and 9-a-side formations](/coaching/best-football-formations-by-age-group) covers the shapes coaches use from here.

That step up shapes which drills earn their place:

- **Fewer touches each, so each one counts for more.** Seven players on a bigger pitch means a child gets the ball less often than at 5v5. A heavy first touch now usually means losing it, with no second chance a yard away.
- **The retreat line rewards playing out.** When the opposition drops back to the halfway line for a goal kick, the defenders and midfielders who can receive the ball calmly and face forward are the ones who get the team up the pitch.
- **Width appears.** On a 37-metre wide pitch there is room on the far side, and children start to learn that the pass across the pitch is often better than the one straight ahead.
- **Positions start to mean something.** Players still rotate, but a 9 year old is now learning what a wide midfielder or a defender does, so drills that put them in different spots help.

None of this means tactics take over. The FA's [age phase priorities](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/england-dna/the-future-england-player/age-phase-priorities) still class everyone up to 11 as Foundation Phase, where players should "stay on the ball, master the ball" and "connect and combine creatively with others". That second part is the new emphasis for this age: the ball skills from 7 and 8 now get used with team-mates.

## Receiving and First Touch Drills

If you only coach one thing at this age, make it receiving. A child who gets the ball under control and facing the right way has time to decide; one who stops it dead under their feet has a defender on them before they look up.

**Gate receiving.** Set up a small gate of two cones, a metre wide, with a passer five or six metres in front of it. The receiver stands just behind the gate, a server passes in, and the receiver takes a first touch through the gate and away. Add a second gate to the left and right so they choose which way to go. Progress it by having a coach stand behind the receiver and step to one side as the pass is played: the receiver has to take the touch away from them.

**Half-turn receiving.** Two players pass across a square of four cones, ten metres per side. The receiver stands side-on, opens their body and takes the first touch with the back foot (the one furthest from the ball) so it travels forward rather than stopping. This is the receiving position that makes the retreat line work: a defender receiving from the goalkeeper side-on can play forward with their second touch.

**Take a picture.** The receiver has to check over their shoulder before the ball arrives, and the coach standing behind them holds up a number of fingers or a coloured cone. The receiver shouts it out, then controls the pass. It builds the habit of looking before receiving, which is where good decisions start. Our guide to [improving football decision-making](/football-development/improve-football-decision-making) covers scanning in more depth.

The most common fault is receiving square-on with both feet planted, so every first touch goes backwards.

## Turning Drills for Getting Out of Pressure

At 9 and 10, a lot of possession is lost in the second after the ball arrives, when a defender is on a child's back. A small number of reliable turns fixes much of that.

England Football's own [scanning and turning session](https://learn.englandfootball.com/sessions/resources/2025/Moving-with-the-ball-and-turning-session-scanning-and-turning), written for 7 to 11 year olds, uses three turns as its examples: the Cruyff turn, the outside hook and the drag-back. Between them they cover turning away from pressure on either side.

**Criss-cross turning.** In a 20 x 20 metre square, two or three groups each have a ball and pass among themselves while moving. Every time a player receives, they have to turn using a different move. Once that is comfortable, take a ball off one group and make them defenders who try to intercept.

**Turn and go.** A player dribbles towards a cone, a coach calls "turn" at a random moment, and the player turns and accelerates back the other way. The point is the acceleration after the turn: plenty of children turn well and then jog, and in a game that lets the defender recover.

Keep the turning circle tight. A turn that swings the ball a metre and a half away from the body works in an empty garden and gets intercepted in a match.

## 1v1 and 2v1 Games

1v1 games are still the single most useful thing at this age, but 9 and 10 year olds are ready for the version that adds a choice: a team-mate.

**1v1 to an end line.** Mark a channel about ten metres wide and fifteen long. The attacker scores by dribbling the ball under control over the defender's end line. The FA's Foundation Phase priorities ask defenders to "be difficult to beat 1v1", so it works both ways: count wins for the defender too, and talk about staying on their feet and showing the attacker one way.

**2v1 to two mini-goals.** Two attackers against one defender, with two small goals at the far end. The attackers can score in either. This is where children learn when to pass and when to keep going, and the answer is usually visible: if the defender comes to you, the team-mate is free; if the defender stays with the team-mate, you go.

A common mistake at this age is a 2v1 where the ball carrier passes the moment the defender is near, every time. That is a habit rather than a decision. Freeze the game once or twice and ask "where was the space?" rather than telling them.

## Passing Drills and Rondos

Passing drills in lines, with children queuing to take turns, give each player very few touches for the time they take up. At 9 and 10, rondos do the same job with far more involvement.

**4v1 rondo.** Four players on the outside of a 10 x 10 metre square, one defender in the middle. The outside players keep the ball; if the defender wins it or the ball leaves the square, whoever lost it swaps in. It builds passing angles, the first touch to set the ball, and moving to give the passer an option.

**5v2 rondo.** The same idea in a slightly bigger square. Two defenders mean a pass straight between them is the prize, so count "splitting passes" as a point.

**Triangle passing with a move.** Three cones in a triangle, one player at each, passing round. After each pass, the passer follows their pass to the next cone. Everyone moves and touches the ball, so it makes a good warm-up.

Two-touch is a good default: one to control, one to pass. Go to one-touch only when the group is keeping the ball comfortably, otherwise the drill becomes a series of mis-hit passes and chasing.

## Small-Sided Games That Do the Coaching

A lot of the teaching at this age can be built into the rules of a game rather than explained at the start.

- **Channel game.** Split the pitch into three channels and say a goal only counts if the ball has been through two of them. Players start using the width without being told to.
- **End-zone game.** Instead of goals, score by receiving the ball in an end zone under control. It rewards a run into space and a pass at the right moment.
- **Retreat line rehearsal.** Restart every attack from your own goalkeeper, with the other team retreating to halfway. It rehearses the exact situation your defenders face at every goal kick on a Saturday.

Smaller games also change how much players look around. A [2025 study of Under 12s at a Category 1 academy](https://research.winchester.ac.uk/en/publications/scanning-differences-between-small-sided-and-full-sized-games-in-/) found that scanning increased as the game format got smaller, across 7v7, 9v9 and 11v11. That supports small-sided games for practising awareness, with the authors' caveat that a small game doesn't fully reproduce what a player sees on a big pitch.

## Drills to Do at Home

Home practice at 9 and 10 can be the same short, regular sessions that work at 7 and 8, with a bit more purpose. Our guide to [ball mastery drills for 7 and 8 year olds](/coaching/football-drills-for-7-and-8-year-olds) still works as the base.

- **Wall receiving with a look.** Pass against a wall, and before each return a parent calls "left" or "right". The child takes their first touch that way. Then swap to a hand signal they have to glance at.
- **Weaker foot only.** Ten minutes of wall passing on the weaker foot. At 7v7, a player who can only receive and pass on one side is easier to defend against, and opponents at this age start to notice.
- **Juggling to a target.** Keep-ups with a number to beat, both feet and thighs. Keep-ups rarely happen in a match, though they are a cheap way to build touch and most children enjoy them at this age.

Ten to fifteen focused minutes is plenty alongside club training. Our guide to [how much training is too much](/football-development/how-much-training-is-too-much) covers the balance if your child is also doing a second club, a development centre or other sports.

## Putting a Team Session Together

Most grassroots sessions at this age run for about an hour. A simple shape that works:

1. **Arrival game (10 minutes).** Something with a ball each that children can join as they turn up, like criss-cross turning or a tag game with dribbling.
2. **Skill practice (15 minutes).** One focus, such as receiving on the half-turn or the 2v1, with one or two coaching points at most.
3. **Rondo or opposed practice (15 minutes).** The same focus with pressure on it.
4. **Game (20 minutes).** A small-sided game with a rule that rewards the focus, and very little stopping.

Keep the focus the same for a few weeks rather than changing it every session. Children at 9 and 10 often need to try something several times, in a game, before it shows up on a Saturday.

## A Drill List to Work From

A short list to glance at before a session or a garden kickabout:

1. Gate receiving, then with a coach stepping to one side.
2. Half-turn receiving across a square, back-foot first touch.
3. Take a picture: look, call the number, receive.
4. Criss-cross turning, one turn per receive.
5. Turn and go, accelerating out of the turn.
6. 1v1 to an end line.
7. 2v1 to two mini-goals.
8. 4v1 rondo, then 5v2.
9. Triangle passing, follow your pass.
10. Channel game or end-zone game to finish.

Two or three of these plus a game make a team session. At home, pick one or two.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What football drills are best for a 9 year old?

Receiving drills that make them take the first touch away from pressure, a couple of reliable turns, and 1v1 games. A 9 year old in Under 10s is in their first 7v7 season, so anything that helps them cope with more space and fewer touches is worth prioritising.

### What football drills are best for a 10 year old?

The same core work, with more choices added: 2v1s, rondos and small-sided games with rules that reward passing and switching play. Most 10 year olds can handle a slightly longer explanation and a drill that progresses within the session.

### Should 9 and 10 year olds be doing fitness or running drills?

Not as separate sessions. Small-sided games and rondos at this age involve plenty of running, and the children are building skill and fitness in the same minutes. Running without a ball takes time away from the touches they need most.

### Is it worth teaching positions at this age?

Lightly. Players benefit from knowing what each position asks of them at 7v7, but rotating them through different positions in training and matches helps more than fixing them in one spot.
