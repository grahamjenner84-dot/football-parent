If you've ever stood on the touchline trying to work out in your head whether your child has had a fair share of the game, you're not alone. Most grassroots coaches want to get this right, but "equal playing time" is easier to say than to actually run on a Sunday morning with ten children, one pitch, and a match that doesn't pause for you to do the maths.

This is written from the coaching side as much as the parent side. It covers what equal playing time actually means, a real method for calculating it, what the rules allow, and how to keep it fair without turning every matchday into an admin exercise.

## What Equal Playing Time Actually Means

Equal playing time means every child in the squad gets a broadly similar amount of time on the pitch across a match, or across a season, rather than the same small group playing every minute while others watch from the side. [England Football's own coaching guidance](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles-and-resources/coaching/resources/2023/What-is-equal-playing-time-in-football) puts it simply: come matchday, everyone in the team gets a fair share of time on the pitch.

The reasoning behind it is straightforward. A child who spends most of a match on the sideline isn't developing, and repeated matches like that tend to affect how much a child enjoys playing at all. It's also, in many age groups, the only way to actually find out which players are capable of more than they're currently being given the chance to show.

That last point matters more than it sounds. [Cornwall FA's guidance on equal playing time](https://www.thefa.com/-/media/cfa/cornwallfa/files/governance/cornwall-fa-guidance--equal-playing-time-2018.ashx) makes the case that children who started playing later, or who are temporarily behind physically or technically, are less likely to catch up if they aren't given the game time and match experience to develop, even though many of them have just as much long-term potential as the players currently ahead of them.

## Equal Minutes vs Equal Position Time

Most guidance on this topic talks about minutes on the pitch. It talks less about where on the pitch those minutes happen, which is its own fairness question. A child who plays 30 minutes but always at right-back is getting a genuinely different experience to a child who plays the same 30 minutes rotating through midfield, defence and attack.

<ParentNote>
As an Under-7s and Under-8s team, we've prioritised equal game time, but we've also tried to make sure it's equal position time. We aim for that over a season rather than match by match, since chasing exact equality every single game can mean a lot of substitutions, which is hard to get right. Some weeks a boy might play two quarters, the next week three. Other teams my son has played for tend to keep the same boys in one or two positions throughout. I think both approaches have their merits: if a boy likes a particular position or finds it easier to learn one role, sticking with it can help. Personally, I think playing multiple positions is better, because they learn to take the ball in different situations with pressure coming from different angles.
</ParentNote>

Neither approach is wrong. A child who's settled and improving in one position is getting real, focused development there. But if the aim is equal opportunity to develop, position rotation is worth building into your thinking alongside minutes, not as an afterthought once the minutes are already sorted.

## How to Calculate Fair Playing Time

This is the part most guidance skips, and it's where a bare online calculator (there are a few, and they do the job) still leaves you without any sense of why the number is what it is.

[Sheffield FA's guidance on planning equal playing time](https://sheffieldfa.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/76000060364-how-to-plan-equal-playing-time-calculating-minutes-) sets out a simple method: take the number of outfield positions in your format, multiply by the length of the match, then divide by the number of outfield players actually available that day.

As a worked example: a 7-a-side match lasting 50 minutes has 6 outfield positions (7 minus the goalkeeper). If you have a squad of 10 and one child plays goalkeeper for the whole match, that leaves 9 outfield players sharing 6 x 50 = 300 outfield-minutes between them, which works out at roughly 33 minutes each. If your goalkeeper also rotates outfield across the season, the maths shifts again: the target minutes per player usually rises slightly, since the whole squad, keeper included, is now sharing both the outfield minutes and the time in goal, rather than one child's minutes being ring-fenced separately.

The number itself matters less than having one. Once you know the target, you can plan substitutions against it rather than guessing on the sideline, and you can see quickly if one child is drifting below it over a run of matches.

## What Rolling Substitutions Actually Allow

Substitution rules vary by age group and format, and they're changing. From the 2026/27 season, the FA's FutureFit reforms move Under-7 football to 3v3, and our [full explainer on the new youth football formats](/football-development/new-fa-youth-football-format) covers the changes in detail. At 3v3, the format is built around continuous involvement for every child rather than a traditional substitutes system, so the "how do we make sure everyone gets a fair go" problem is largely designed out of that specific age group rather than left to the coach to solve. Check [England Football's FutureFit pages](https://futurefit.englandfootball.com/futurefit/index.html) for the current detail at your child's age group, since formats are being phased in and local leagues may implement the timeline slightly differently.

Rolling substitutions aren't just a mini-soccer feature. The FA's [Standard Code of Rules for Youth Competitions](https://www.thefa.com/~/media/Files/TheFAPortal/governance-docs/rules-of-the-association/standard-code-of-rules-for-youth-competitions.ashx) allows roll-on, roll-off substitutions across youth football generally, including 9v9 and youth 11-a-side, meaning a player can come off and go back on more than once during a match, usually with the referee's permission and at a stoppage in play. Individual leagues and competitions can set their own limits on top of that. This is where equal playing time genuinely depends on the coach actively managing it, since the rules create the opportunity for fair rotation but don't enforce it. A formation that only really works with one specific set of players makes that harder to pull off. Our guide to [7-a-side and 9-a-side formations](/coaching/best-football-formations-by-age-group) covers picking a shape that still rotates fairly.

## Tracking Playing Time Without Extra Matchday Admin

Knowing the target number is one thing. Actually tracking who's had what, mid-match, while also running the game, is the part that trips coaches up.

<ParentNote>
We used a spreadsheet on our phone. That's actually what led me to building the [Coach App](/football-parent-coach-app): to make it easier to auto-generate a team, get warned if you'd broken your own team's rotation rules, and log and track goals and assists more easily on the day.
</ParentNote>

Whatever method you use, whether that's a paper team sheet with tally marks, a spreadsheet, or something built for the job, the useful part isn't the tool itself. It's having your rotation rules written down before kick-off, rather than deciding fairness on the fly at half time. If you're also planning what a session actually looks like at younger ages, our guide to [ball mastery drills for 7 and 8 year olds](/coaching/football-drills-for-7-and-8-year-olds) covers session structure at the age where rotation habits often get set.

## Keeping Rotation Transparent and Fair

The biggest risk with any rotation system isn't the maths, it's the perception that it isn't being followed. If parents can't see how decisions are being made, "equal playing time" starts to look like whatever the coach decides on the day.

<ParentNote>
It's never really been a problem for us, because equal playing time is club policy and the team is shared with parents before the game, which makes it transparent. That also helps avoid any accusation of bias, that our own sons are playing more than others, or always playing up front and that's why they're scoring more. I think that kind of accusation is common in some grassroots environments. On other teams, I've seen parents put more pressure on coaches, or complain about playing time or where their child was playing. From what I've seen, it's a bit chicken and egg: players who get more game time or more periods tend to grow into the game, but they also develop more and feel more confidence from their coach.
</ParentNote>

Sharing the team and the rotation plan before the game, rather than announcing substitutions as they happen, removes most of the guesswork for parents watching from the side. It's a small habit, but it does the heavy lifting.

## Does Equal Playing Time Change as Kids Get Older?

<ParentNote>
For me, it's probably always the case that a child should get at least half a game if they're committed and part of the squad. But as they get older, I think it becomes more about earning their time and working hard to keep it. In the younger age groups, they're generally all trying their best, but some take a little longer to develop. That doesn't mean they won't catch up, and sometimes overtake, the stars of Under-7s, if they're given the right environment to develop.
</ParentNote>

This tracks with the wider development picture. Our guide to [late developers in football](/football-development/late-developers-in-football) covers why a child who's behind at 7 or 8 is often not behind for the reasons parents assume, and why physical or technical gaps at that age tend to close as children mature at different rates. Equal playing time in the younger age groups is partly what makes that catching-up possible in the first place: a child who never gets game time never gets the chance to show it.

## Talking to Your Child's Coach About Playing Time

If your child's team doesn't have a clear rotation policy, or you're unsure whether one exists, it's worth asking directly rather than assuming. A calm, specific question, such as how the club approaches playing time across a season, tends to get a more useful answer than a complaint raised after a match your child didn't enjoy. Our guide on [talking to your child's coach after a bad match](/parent-guides/support-child-after-bad-match) covers how to keep these conversations constructive from the coach's side as well as the parent's, which is exactly the balance a playing-time conversation needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should kids get equal playing time in cup or tournament matches?

Many clubs relax their usual rotation policy for cup or tournament football, where the result carries more weight for the group as a whole. This is a genuine trade-off rather than a rule, and it's worth a club or coach being upfront with parents about when and why it happens, rather than it becoming a surprise on the day.

### Can a player refuse a substitution?

Under the [Laws of the Game](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-players/), a player who refuses to leave the pitch when substituted doesn't stop the match, but they can be cautioned if they excessively delay the restart. Grassroots youth football doesn't use the formal substitution-board procedure that rule was written for, but the underlying principle holds at every level: once a coach makes the call, the game moves on around it. If a child is regularly unhappy about being substituted, that's usually worth a conversation about how and why rotation decisions are being made, rather than treating it as a one-off complaint.

### What age does rolling substitution stop applying?

This varies by league and format rather than following one fixed national age. As children move into larger formats and older age groups, substitution rules typically become more structured and closer to the adult game, with fewer, more deliberate changes rather than continuous rolling subs. Check your own league's rules for the exact age your child's format changes.

### Is equal playing time used in academy football?

Academy environments generally move away from strict equal playing time earlier than grassroots football, since selection and playing time become more closely tied to development plans for each individual player, though this varies by club and academy phase rather than following one single published rule the way grassroots guidance does. That's a different context to grassroots, where the wider aim is participation and development for the whole squad rather than a smaller group being prepared for a specific pathway.

### What if my team doesn't have enough players for genuine rotation?

A small squad limits how much you can rotate, but the target-minutes approach still works: it just means the gap between your most and least played children may be harder to close in any single match. Tracking it over a run of matches rather than game by game, as covered above, matters even more when squad numbers are tight.
