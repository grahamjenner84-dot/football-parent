## Academy Life: A Conversation With Paul Barry

Most academy guides explain the system from the outside: what a Category 1 club looks like on paper, what the rules say, what the pathway is meant to do. Far fewer come from someone who has actually sat across the table from families at every level of it.

Football Parent spoke to **Paul Barry**, Head of Coaching, Content & Club Support at Football DNA, about what academy life is actually like once a family is inside it: how clubs at different categories treat parents, whether climbing to a Category 1 academy is always the right move, juggling grassroots alongside an academy programme, whether development centres genuinely lead anywhere, what to focus on in the early years, and how to support a child through release.

## Family Treatment: Category 1 vs Lower Category Academies

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Having worked at clubs across different levels, from Southend up to Arsenal and Crystal Palace, what's the biggest difference in how families are treated at a smaller academy versus a Category 1 academy?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

The sheer difference in staff numbers, and therefore departmental provision, allows Category 1 academies a greater scope to support parents and families compared to lower category clubs. Player Care has become a very prominent role in academies, with some clubs even having age-phase related staff responsible for different age groups within a programme.

With more staff, more care and attention can be provided, as Player Care staff work alongside coaching and safeguarding colleagues to address issues when, or before, they arise, conduct parent meetings whenever needed, and both guide and educate families to first understand the academy journey, and hopefully enjoy it (despite the challenges faced) along the way.

## Is Category 1 Always the Best Move?

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Is getting into a Category 1 academy always the best move, or are there situations where a lower category, or a different pathway altogether, actually serves a player better?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

From my experience, every player's journey is different, and therefore it's not a "one size fits all" development approach. Some players embrace the pressure and challenges posed by a Premier League Category 1 academy, and others struggle initially before developing the confidence (on and off the pitch) and personality to manage their emotions and deal with the competitive nature of the environment.

Sometimes, registering for a lower category club develops more humility, resilience and greater inner drive and motivation. This may be because the club is considered an "underdog" without the financial backing and support. This often means that staff have to fulfil more than one role, develop a greater sense of unity, and work hard together to ensure that players enjoy the experience, develop every season, and maintain the motivation to represent the club.

## Can a Family Juggle Grassroots and Academy Football?

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Have you seen a family successfully juggle grassroots and academy football at the same time? What actually made that work?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

I have witnessed this in terms of two or more siblings, where one may have registered for an academy, with their brothers and/or sisters playing grassroots football. The Premier League allows boys in academies from the age of U9s, U10s and U11s to play grassroots at the same time as academy football. This initiative was created to give the youngest players opportunities to retain friendships, be the "better" player where the level is not as high or competitive, and continue playing the game for social reasons.

However, there are hardly any parents that I've seen take up this opportunity, probably through fear of "losing face" and their son missing out or being considered inferior to their teammates at the academy.

## Are Development Centres a Genuine Route Into the Academy?

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
Having worked across foundation-phase programmes at more than one club, are development centres a genuine route into the academy proper, or are they mostly a separate system that rarely feeds into it? Does that differ much club to club?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

Both Southend United and Crystal Palace had, or have, very productive development centres (called Shadow Squads or Showcase) which genuinely gave boys opportunities to trial throughout each season. Fixtures were often arranged between development centre teams and academy age groups, and quite often these games were competitive.

Of course, this differs from club to club, with other programmes potentially being more formally in place as a mandatory element through either the EFL or the Premier League.

## What Should Parents Focus On in the Foundation Years?

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
In the foundation years, what would you focus on as a parent to give your child the best chance of opportunities as they get older?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

It's important that parents and guardians try to embrace every aspect of the academy journey. There are likely to be far more challenging and difficult moments compared to relative successes, so they need to be there emotionally for their child, give encouragement and positive reinforcement, and, importantly, leave the coaching to the club's coaches.

Ongoing education for parents is vital, as it is easy to agree with an academy's code of conduct and values at the start of the U9s season (the first registration year), but by the time they reach U10s and U11s, to have lost sight of what is most important. We all know that the probability of boys in academies being retained through every age group, each season, gaining a scholarship and progressing forwards with a first-team contract, is extremely slim.

Therefore, parents need to understand and appreciate every opportunity their son experiences. They'll play against some of the best teams, visit amazing training grounds, play in competitive tournaments, and possibly even travel abroad on tours. The life lessons formed through these experiences should be where parents focus, rather than constantly comparing their child with others and questioning the club's opinions and direction, all of which can have a major detrimental effect on their child in the long term.

## Supporting a Child Through Academy Release

<div className="mb-8 rounded-2xl border border-slate-200 bg-slate-50 px-6 py-5">

<p className="mb-3 text-sm font-semibold text-blue-700">
Football Parent asks
</p>

<p className="m-0 text-slate-700">
What's one thing you wish more parents understood about supporting a child through release?
</p>

</div>

<p className="mb-5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.15em] text-slate-500">
Paul Barry • Football DNA
</p>

Every decision made comes after months of collective deliberation involving multiple staff members, including coaches, sports scientists, player care, safeguarding, recruitment and senior staff. The decision to release a player is a club decision, not an individual one, and is made with their best interests at heart. Parents need to accept and respect the club's decision and, most importantly, be there emotionally for their child. This isn't a time to criticise the club, especially in front of their child, but instead an opportunity to act with maturity, self-awareness and high-level emotional intelligence.

I've known many boys who have been released, listened to the club's feedback, and gone on to trial elsewhere, sign at other clubs and thrive in an environment that clearly suited them more appropriately. Therefore, a release shouldn't be thought of as the final moment in a young player's journey, but instead a crucial learning moment that can actually deliver a vital life lesson, allow them an opportunity to self-reflect, and eventually help them find an environment that aligns with their level and future ambitions.

## Final Thoughts

Across these six answers, Paul's view keeps returning to the same idea: the academy system rewards families who judge it on their own child's fit and progress, not on category labels, comparisons with other players, or how a decision looks from the outside.

A [Category 1 academy](/academy-pathway/academy-categories-explained) isn't automatically the right environment for every player. Playing grassroots alongside an academy programme is [allowed but rarely taken up](/academy-pathway/can-academy-players-play-grassroots-football), mostly for reasons of perception rather than rules. [Development centres](/academy-pathway/development-centres-vs-academies) can be a genuine route in, but the route looks different from club to club. And [release](/academy-pathway/understanding-academy-release), when it comes, is a collective decision made with the player's welfare in mind, not a final judgement on their potential.
